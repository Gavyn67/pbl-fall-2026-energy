The problem: demand is rising and bills are too

Natural rights and the purpose of government

The Declaration of Independence says rights are not handed out by a government. People are born with them, and governments exist to secure them. John Locke’s social contract says the same: people consent to government so that it protects life, liberty, and property.

The hard question is the tension at the center of this project. A natural right is not automatically a government service, and the Constitution never names energy, housing, food, or healthcare. Our answer is that electricity is the condition for exercising rights we already have. Without it, a student cannot study at night, a family cannot keep food or medicine cold, and a worker cannot join a modern economy.

Literature connection: Into the Wild

Jon Krakauer’s book shows how independence and survival depend on knowing your environment and having the resources to live in it. Chris McCandless’s story is a reminder that freedom without access to essentials is fragile. Energy is the essential resource of modern life.

Four current stories, one pattern

California expands battery storage (Aug. 7, 2026)

The state reported 21,112 megawatts of battery storage. Government planning and investment can make clean electricity available when demand is high, but it takes infrastructure, not just a declaration.

California requires 6,000 MW of new clean capacity (Mar. 2, 2026)

The California Public Utilities Commission approved a decision requiring regulated providers to procure 6,000 megawatts of new clean-energy and storage capacity. Public regulation can make sure enough power exists as demand grows.

States sue over offshore wind agreements

California, New York, and other states sued the federal administration over agreements involving payments to offshore wind developers to abandon projects. The administration said investment could go to other sources; the states said the deals were unlawful and could harm energy supply and climate goals. Energy policy changes with political leadership even though households depend on steady power.

PJM moves to track data centers (Oct. 12, 2026)

US regulators said PJM Interconnection can begin building a database to track data centers, whose rapid expansion has raised prices and strained reliability. Who pays for new grid infrastructure is now a core fairness question.

Who should be responsible? The federalism analysis

Suppose Congress guarantees every household a minimum amount of affordable, clean electricity. The system works only if each level does its job.

ActorResponsibility
Federal governmentNational minimum standards, interstate grid regulation, funding for low-income households
State governmentsRegulate utilities, write energy plans, encourage renewables and storage
Local governmentsCommunity solar, efficient public buildings, helping residents find assistance
Private companiesGenerate and deliver power, maintain lines, pay a fair share of grid costs

When federal and state policies conflict, a valid federal law wins under the Supremacy Clause. Congress can regulate interstate power grids and pipelines through the Commerce Clause, and in McCulloch v. Maryland the Supreme Court read the Necessary and Proper Clause to give Congress implied powers to carry out its enumerated ones. States keep the freedom to go further than the federal minimum, so the floor is national and the ceiling is local.

Why people who agree energy matters still disagree

Conservative

Prioritizes reliability, lower costs, and domestic production. The Trump administration has favored fossil-fuel production and rolled back support for some clean-energy projects.

Liberal

Prioritizes consumer protection, aid for low-income households, and clean-energy investment, as in federal clean-energy tax incentives and California’s affordability programs.

Libertarian

Favors individual choice, free markets, and fewer regulations, and would oppose a federally guaranteed energy entitlement.

Progressive

Treats energy as a basic necessity and pushes for public investment in renewables, utility assistance, and protection for disadvantaged communities.

A progressive may say government must guarantee affordable clean power. A conservative may say competition should set prices, with limited help for those in need. No single policy satisfies both, so any real plan needs trade-offs.

Our answer: yes, and here is the policy

Access to clean and affordable energy should be treated as a natural right. Our Energy Access Program guarantees a basic level of access instead of controlling every energy decision.

  1. Congress guarantees a minimum

    Every household gets access to a minimum amount of reliable, affordable electricity, with federal funding to states and national consumer-protection standards.

  2. States run it

    States regulate utilities, write energy plans, expand renewables and storage, and operate bill-assistance programs.

  3. Localities connect people

    Cities support community solar, upgrade public buildings, and help residents enroll in assistance.

  4. Big users pay their share

    Large energy users such as data centers cover the infrastructure costs their demand creates, so costs are not shifted onto households.

  5. Protect affordability and reliability

    Rules against unreasonable disconnections, regular rate reviews, and investment in power lines, storage, and a diverse energy mix.

Funding: a mix of federal and state money, assistance programs, and fair contributions from large users. Limits: transparent budgets, clear standards, and states free to choose their own energy sources.

Counterargument, rebuttal, and constitutional limits

The counterargument

The Constitution does not list electricity as a right, and Congress cannot create unlimited power just by calling something a natural right. Opponents also warn of higher federal spending and interference in private markets. U.S. v. Lopez shows the Court will police the limits of the Commerce Clause.

The rebuttal

Our program does not claim a new constitutional right. It rests on enumerated powers: regulating interstate commerce, taxing, and spending for the general welfare. Electric grids cross state lines and are economic activity, which is exactly what Lopez says Congress may regulate. A guaranteed minimum plus targeted aid is also a realistic compromise, not free power for all.

Who would support it: progressives, liberals, and environmental groups like the Sierra Club. Who would oppose it: conservatives, libertarians, and some energy companies. Public support rises when bills, outages, or pollution hit home, which is why a targeted, state-run design has the best chance of passing.

The bottom line

Electricity is the condition for living safely and using the rights we already have. Under natural-rights and social-contract principles, government has a duty to protect access to it, through national standards, state management, and fair costs. A right to power means a guaranteed floor, not government control of the grid.
